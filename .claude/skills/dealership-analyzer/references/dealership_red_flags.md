# Dealership red-flag checklist

Common shady used-car dealer practices, compiled from consumer-protection and legal sources. Apply during Step 2 (site analysis) and Step 6 (final report) — note any of these observed directly on the dealer's own site/listings, and any reported by reviewers/complaints in Steps 3-5.

## Pricing & fees
- **Doc fee far above the state norm/cap.** Some states (e.g. California, New York) cap documentation fees; others don't. An unusually high doc fee, or one not disclosed until late in the process, is a flag.
- **Fake or inflated line-item fees** — "dealer prep," "reconditioning," "market adjustment" — padded beyond the real cost of the described service.
- **Advertised price that excludes mandatory fees/add-ons**, discovered only once you're at the dealership.
- **Add-on packages** (paint/fabric protection, VIN etching, rustproofing, nitrogen tire fill) presented as mandatory rather than optional. Under FTC rules, written consent is required for add-ons — a dealer that bundles them in without a clear opt-out is a flag.

## Sales tactics
- **Bait-and-switch**: an attractively priced advertised vehicle turns out to be "just sold" or "unavailable" once you arrive, and you're steered to a pricier unit.
- **High-pressure/urgency tactics** — "gone by tomorrow," pushing signature before the buyer has reviewed terms.
- **Refusal to put terms in writing**, or reluctance to provide a full paperwork breakdown before signing.
- **Yo-yo financing / spot delivery**: buyer drives off before financing is actually finalized, then is called back days later and told the loan "fell through," pressured into a worse rate or higher payment, sometimes threatened with repossession or losing the trade-in if they resist. Renegotiated rates average ~5 points higher than the original quote.

## Inventory sourcing
- **Curbstoning signals**: multiple listings that share the same contact phone/email despite claiming to be private-party sales, refusal to provide official paperwork, insistence on cash payment, unwillingness to meet at a fixed business address. Curbstoning is a common route for flood/salvage/rolled-back-odometer vehicles to re-enter the market disguised as private-party sales.
- **Vehicle history gaps that don't match the dealer's own claims** (e.g. dealer claims "one owner" but the vehicle's own site copy or a spot-checked Carfax shows otherwise) — cross-check against Step 7's optional spot-check if one was run.

## Warranty & "as-is" sales
- Under the FTC Used Car Rule, every used vehicle must display a Buyers Guide window sticker disclosing warranty terms (or "as is" status) — its absence, or terms that contradict what a salesperson says verbally, is a flag.
- **If the Buyers Guide discloses a warranty, "as is" language in the contract does not override it** — a dealer that tries to deny warranty coverage by pointing to "as is" contract language despite a disclosed warranty is violating the rule.
- **Denying warranty coverage because a third party did routine maintenance/repairs** is illegal under federal law unless the dealer can show that specific repair caused the failure — a pattern of this in reviews/complaints is a flag on the dealer's service department, not just a one-off dispute.

## Reputation-level signals (cross-reference with Steps 3-5)
- Reviews or complaints repeatedly describing the same tactic above (e.g. multiple people describing a doc-fee surprise, or a yo-yo financing callback) — a pattern across independent reviewers is stronger signal than a single complaint.
- BBB complaints tab showing a volume inconsistent with the letter grade (see `general-dealership-review-sites.md`).
- News coverage or AG actions naming any of these specific practices.

None of these, individually, proves fraud — dealers can have a single bad review or one disputed fee. The signal is a **pattern**: the same practice recurring across multiple independent sources (site language + reviews + complaints + news), not an isolated incident.
